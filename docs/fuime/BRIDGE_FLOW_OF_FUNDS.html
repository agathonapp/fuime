§ 1

The two answers, up front

Will received funds be exchanged for another currency?

No foreign currency is involved at any point. Every amount is denominated in US dollars from the customer's card to the family's bank account. There is one conversion inside Bridge, and it is USD into a USD-pegged stablecoin at 1:1 on receipt, converted back to USD before funds leave. No FX rate is ever applied, no non-USD currency is held, and no party to the flow is outside the United States.

Where will funds ultimately be sent?

To two destinations, both US bank accounts. (1) The venture's payout account — held by the parent or guardian, the teen operator, or jointly — approved by the guardian and verified by Plaid Auth before the first payout. (2) Ninth Street Labs' own operating account, for the platform fee it earned as seller. Nothing is sent to a wallet address, to a non-US account, or to any end user in stablecoin.

The rest of this document supports those two answers: who the parties are (§2), the path money takes (§3–4), the conversion in detail (§5), the destinations in detail (§6), why Ninth Street Labs treats this as its own revenue rather than third-party funds (§7), the controls on the payout leg (§8), and four questions we would rather ask than assume (§9).

§ 5

Currency exchange, in detail

Two instruments touch the flow and one of them is a 1:1 claim on the other. Stated precisely:

  • No foreign currency, ever. Every party is in the United States. Buyers are US retail customers, ventures are US businesses, payout accounts are US bank accounts. No FX rate is applied at any leg and there is no FX exposure to disclose.
  • Two conversions, both 1:1, both inside Bridge. USD into a USD-pegged stablecoin on receipt, and back to USD before origination. Funds are in stablecoin only for the interval between settlement and the next approved payout run — currently a weekly cycle with a 7-day minimum hold.
  • The venture's claim is denominated in dollars, not in the stablecoin. This is the important one. The venture is owed a fixed US dollar amount. If the stablecoin were to trade away from par, Ninth Street Labs would still owe the full dollar payable and would make up the difference from its own funds. Conversion and de-peg risk sit entirely with Ninth Street Labs.
  • No end user holds, sees, or is offered stablecoin. Families are shown a dollar amount owed and a date, and they receive dollars by ACH. Neither the teen operator nor the guardian has a wallet, a key, or any ability to direct funds on-chain.
  • No yield. No interest or yield is earned on balances held in conversion, and none is credited to ventures. See §9 for the confirmation we are asking Bridge for on this point.

Explicitly out of scope, by design

These are not unimplemented features awaiting a roadmap — they are excluded because each one would convert a vendor payable into stored value, and are enforced as a standing prohibition in our payout runbook:

  • No spendable platform balance. A venture cannot draw on the figure it is owed.
  • No peer-to-peer transfers between ventures or users.
  • No card issued against a payable.
  • No disbursement in stablecoin or any other crypto asset, and no wallet addresses as payout destinations.
  • No international payouts and no non-US destination accounts.
  • No payout without a linked guardian obligor and a guardian-approved destination.
§ 7

Why Ninth Street Labs treats this as its own revenue

Bridge's question is headed third-party money movement, so this is the part worth being direct about.

Ninth Street Labs is the seller, not an intermediary. The customer's contract of sale is with Ninth Street Labs. Ninth Street Labs' name is on the checkout page, the receipt and the terms of sale; Ninth Street Labs sets the terms of sale, honours the 14-day refund window out of its own funds, and is the party a cardholder disputes against. Money received is therefore Ninth Street Labs' own revenue, and paying the venture afterwards is Ninth Street Labs paying one of its own vendors. This is the standard merchant-of-record structure operated by Paddle, FastSpring, Lemon Squeezy and Whop.

The per-venture accounts are segregation, not custody. Ninth Street Labs is the account holder on each one. The balance sitting in a venture's account is Ninth Street Labs' money; what the venture holds is a US dollar claim against Ninth Street Labs, not a claim on any particular account or on the funds in it. One account per venture exists because it makes reconciliation, audit and dispute attribution far cleaner than a single commingled pool — it is a bookkeeping choice, not a custodial one.

What a venture cannot do is what keeps this true. A venture cannot spend from the figure, move it to another user, draw a card against it, direct where it is held, or hold it indefinitely. Any one of those would make it stored value regardless of what it is called, and each is prohibited in our payout runbook rather than merely unbuilt.

Disclosed rather than assumed

Ninth Street Labs is not registered as an MSB with FinCEN and holds no state money transmitter licences, on the basis of the own-revenue characterisation above. A state-by-state money transmission analysis has not been completed, and the structure has not yet been reviewed by outside counsel. We would rather Bridge have that on the record at underwriting than discover it later.

§ 8

Controls on the payout leg

Under merchant of record, a disputed sale is disputed against Ninth Street Labs and the acquirer debits Ninth Street Labs — not the venture. The card dispute window is 120 days and payouts are weekly, so the controls below exist to bound that gap without making a teenager wait four months to be paid.

Standing controls
ControlSettingPurpose
Hold from settlement7 days Catches the fast failures — a stolen card, a same-week chargeback — before money leaves.
Rolling reserve10% Sized against trailing volume, so it scales with the exposure it covers. Roughly twenty times cover against the dispute rate a vetted services business should see.
Reserve window90 days The trailing period the reserve target is computed over. A venture that stops trading sees the reserve unwind to zero over one window.
Per-venture cap$2,500 / run A concentration limit, not a refusal — the remainder rolls to the next run. A venture whose volume stops resembling the business we vetted cannot clear the account before a human looks.
Minimum payout$10 Below the floor the payable rolls forward rather than generating a transfer.
Human approvalevery run No batch pays out without a named approver and a timestamp. Policy values are frozen onto the batch at generation so a run can always be explained after the fact.

§ 9

Four questions for Bridge

  1. Account topology. We have specified one Bridge account per venture because it makes reconciliation and audit cleaner. If Bridge's underwriting reads a per-end-user account as a customer-designated account — custody of a third party's funds rather than segregation of ours — we would rather know now and hold a single Ninth Street Labs account with internal sub-ledgering instead. The economic substance is identical either way; only the topology differs, and we lose nothing operationally by changing it.
  2. KYB scope. Given the accounts hold Ninth Street Labs' own funds, does Bridge require know-your-business diligence on each venture, or only on Ninth Street Labs as the account holder? This materially affects our onboarding flow, because our operators are minors and their guardians are the adult parties.
  3. Timing we can promise families. What is the reliable end-to-end time from an approved payout instruction to funds landing in a family's account, including both conversions? We would rather quote a date we can hit than Bridge's best case.
  4. Yield and returned items. Confirmation that no yield accrues on balances held between conversions, and Bridge's handling of a returned or failed ACH on the payout leg — specifically whether funds return as USD or as stablecoin.
